Building the Architecture Diagram

This week followed one order through four checkpoints and then took the systems apart to see what carries it between them. Here you rebuild the architecture yourself, and then take an error apart stage by stage.

Informatics in Pharmacy Practice · 1213404 Maps to sessions 3.1 and 3.2 Learning outcomes K1 · S1 Approximately 95 minutes
Before you start

Section G is the one to spend real time on. The mid-term asks you to analyse a medication safety case using the four-step method, and the marks are distributed across all four steps rather than concentrated in the solution. Nothing here is stored, so print the completed module or note your errors as you go.

A

Warm-up: what you must already know

8 min

Five statements from this week that carry disproportionate examination weight. Read each, then cover it and reconstruct it before moving on.

Session 3.1 · Stage 2

Verification is the only independent check

Stage 2 is the only stage whose entire purpose is to check work already done. Removing it does not remove one check among five; it removes the only independent one. Its absence is therefore far more dangerous than it appears on the diagram.

Session 3.1 · Stage 4

Barcode administration cannot judge an order

It verifies identity and product against the order as recorded, and makes no clinical judgement. If the recorded order is itself wrong, every downstream checkpoint confirms the error rather than catching it. This distinction appears in almost every case question on stage 4.

Session 3.1 · Closed loop

Closed-loop is a property of the connections

A hospital can own every technology on the diagram and still run an open loop, because owning the components and connecting them are different projects. The 50 to 70 per cent error reduction comes from the arrows between the boxes, not from the boxes.

Session 3.2 · Interfaces

An interface is a contract, not a shared database

It defines the data format, the access rules, and the structure of the request and the response. Neither system is changed by it, which is precisely why two products from two vendors built a decade apart can be joined at all.

The fifth statement

A workaround is evidence that the workflow was never redesigned, not that the staff are careless. Scanning at the nursing station rather than the bedside is a signal about wireless coverage or screen placement. Remove the cause and the workaround disappears; leave the cause in place and it survives any amount of retraining.

Where the marks are

Why this exercise matters

Once the components are placed, trace the five data exchanges across them: the order is created, routed to pharmacy, verified and dispensed, administered and documented, then monitored with the finding fed back. Those five exchanges map exactly onto the five stages of the medication-use process — the stages describe what happens clinically, the exchanges describe what has to move for it to happen.

Terminology matching

10 min

Each term takes exactly one definition, and each definition is used once. Several distractors are true statements about a neighbouring term.

TermDefinition
Where the marks are

The pair most often confused is adverse drug event against medication error. Every error that causes harm is an adverse event, but so is an expected adverse effect from a correctly prescribed and correctly administered dose — so the two sets overlap without either containing the other. Expect a stem that describes a correct dose producing a known side effect and asks you to classify it.

D

Complete the data flow

10 min

Two structured recall exercises. Type directly into the blanks; capitalisation and punctuation are ignored.

D1 · Five exchanges, five failure points

Word bank: created · routed · verified · administered · monitored · acknowledgement

Exchange 1. The order is by the clinician in CPOE.

Exchange 2. The order is automatically to the pharmacy system.

Exchange 3. The order is by the pharmacist, then dispensed by cabinet or robot.

Exchange 4. The dose is after a barcode scan, and the record is updated.

Exchange 5. The response is through labs, vital signs and alerts.

At every interface an message tells the sender that the receiver actually received what was sent.

D2 · Five implementation success factors

Word bank: leadership · interdisciplinary · champions · workflow · optimisation

Factor 1. commitment — senior leaders publicly champion the project and allocate budget and staff time.

Factor 2. involvement — pharmacy, nursing, medicine, IT, finance and legal from the first day.

Factor 3. Clinical — front-line clinicians who persuade resistant colleagues by peer influence.

Factor 4. redesign first — you cannot place an electronic system on top of a broken paper process.

Factor 5. Continuous — go-live is the start, and new error types peak in weeks one to twelve.

Where the marks are

Four of the five implementation factors are about people and process rather than software. If a question asks why an implementation failed and one option blames the software while three describe human or organisational causes, the software option is almost always the distractor. Workflow redesign first is the factor most often omitted from student answers.

G

Error propagation workbench

15 min

Two cases requiring the four-step method in full. Write your own answer in each field before revealing the model response, and compare the structure as well as the content — the structure is what is marked.

Case 1 · The insulin tenfold overdose
A hospitalised patient receives 100 units of insulin subcutaneously; the intended dose was 10 units. The prescriber selected the wrong entry from a CPOE dropdown. The pharmacist verified the order. The cabinet released the full quantity. The nurse scanned the wristband and the product successfully before administering. The patient develops severe hypoglycaemia and is admitted to intensive care.
Model response
  • Identify. Stage 1 is where the error entered, and stages 2, 3 and 4 are where it should have been intercepted and was not. Stage 5 is where the harm was detected, which is not a failure of stage 5 — it did its job, too late to help. Naming only stage 1 is a partial answer worth partial marks.
  • Map, stage 1. CPOE with decision support was present. The failure mode is a dropdown selection error — a new error type created by electronic ordering, which handwriting could not produce. No insulin dose ceiling was configured, so nothing blocked the entry.
  • Map, stage 2. Pharmacist verification with an enhanced rules engine was present. Alert fatigue from excessive non-specific warnings had produced reflexive overriding of all alerts, so the independent check was functionally absent.
  • Map, stage 3. The automated cabinet was present but no insulin-specific quantity limit had been configured in its drug library, so it released the full amount without objection.
  • Map, stage 4. Barcode administration worked correctly. It confirmed the right patient and the right drug against the order as recorded, and the order as recorded said 100 units. It makes no clinical judgement, so it permitted the dose.
  • Locate and design. A decision support hard stop for insulin above 50 units at stage 1; insulin-specific verification rules at stage 2 that cannot be cleared with a single reflexive click; a quantity limit in the cabinet drug library for high-alert medicines at stage 3; an independent nurse double-check for insulin at stage 4, since scanning cannot assess the order; a surveillance rule checking blood glucose after any large insulin dose at stage 5; and system-wide reduction of alert fatigue, because it was the common cause linking the failures at stages 2 and 3.
  • Marks are lost by proposing only the stage 1 hard stop. Layered defence works only when the layers are independent, and the question is testing whether you have understood that alert fatigue had already correlated two of them with each other.
Case 2 · The silent renal alert
A district hospital runs a well-configured pharmacy system containing a complete set of renal dosing rules. Over six months, no renal dose adjustment alert has ever fired, and two patients with impaired renal function have received standard vancomycin doses. The laboratory system holds correct and current creatinine values for both patients. The pharmacy system is not interfaced to the laboratory system, and results reach the ward as printed reports.
Model response
  • Why no alert fired. Decision support requires both a knowledge-based rule and patient-specific data. The rule was present and correct; the creatinine was present and correct; but they were held in two systems with no interface between them. The rule could not run at all, because the data never reached the system holding the rule.
  • The distinction that carries the marks. An alert that never fired is an architecture problem. An alert that fired and was overridden is a human factors problem. They look similar in an incident report and have entirely different solutions — an interface in the first case, alert redesign in the second. Answers that describe pharmacist override score badly here, because the stem gives no alert to override.
  • The comparison illustrated. Standalone against integrated systems, and specifically the decision support row: a standalone system can screen only against what it holds itself. The other rows are also in play — data sharing is manual by printed report, and there are no real-time updates — but the decision support row is what produced the harm.
  • The fix. Interface the laboratory system to the pharmacy system so that results flow automatically, with acknowledgement messages confirming receipt and an audit log at the interface. Verify that the creatinine field the rules engine reads is the one the interface populates, since a rule reading an empty field behaves exactly as it did before.
  • What it does not fix. It does not address whether the rule thresholds are clinically right, whether the alerts will be actionable when they start firing, or whether the sudden arrival of a new alert stream will produce alert fatigue. Building the interface converts a silent system into a noisy one, and the noise then has to be designed.

Focus your revision here

The five items below account for most of the marks lost on Week 3 material. Each is a discrimination rather than a fact, which is why re-reading the slides does not fix them — you have to practise choosing between the two options.

  1. An alert that never fired against one that was overridden. The first is an architecture problem and the second is a human factors problem. Read the stem for whether an alert was generated at all before you choose.
  2. What each stage-4 technology can and cannot judge. Barcode administration verifies identity and product against the recorded order. It has no view of whether that order should have been written, so it cannot be the answer to a wrong-order question.
  3. Owning the components against connecting them. A hospital with every technology on the diagram can still run an open loop. When a stem lists impressive technology and describes harm anyway, look at the links.
  4. Break in the loop, identified from the missing alert category. Missing administration data points to a paper record. Silent renal overdoses point to an unintegrated laboratory feed. Each break silences a different and identifiable set of rules.
  5. Workarounds as system signals. When a stem describes staff bypassing a control, the correct answer changes the system to fit the work. Options beginning with “retrain” or “report” are almost always the distractors.
